# Outline, drafting, and revision

## Shared writing principles

Apply these during paragraph drafting, local revision, and the final read-through. They also apply when no sample papers are used.

### Write a research argument

Use the technical reports and code for facts. Use the outline to select and organize the manuscript. Explain the research question, method idea, evidence, and significance. Do not translate the technical report or code order into a manuscript, enumerate every implementation step, or expand each configuration field into prose. Essential implementation detail should have a clear role in understanding or evaluating the work.

### Avoid defensive prose

State what the evidence supports directly. Do not anticipate every possible objection with repeated disclaimers, unvalidated possibilities, or self-negating sentences. Most general limitations belong in a concise appropriate discussion. Preserve short conditions that materially affect a claim's validity or scope; removing defensive padding must not expand the claim.

Keep the agent's unresolved verification questions in workspace notes or a focused question to the author. Do not pour an internal audit checklist into the paper. The manuscript need not pursue absolute logical completeness through repetitive explanation.

### Use plain, accurate English

Prefer familiar words, concrete verbs, and readily understood syntax while preserving technical precision. Split sentences carrying too many ideas. Avoid decorative vocabulary, excessive nominalization, and layers of subordinate clauses. Do not enforce a universal word count per sentence or delete a necessary condition merely to make a sentence shorter.

### Select detail and equations

Include what the section needs for its purpose, understanding the main idea, assessing evidence, and necessary reproducibility. Other detail can remain in the technical report or supplemental material. Briefly describe and cite standard methods; explain new, modified, or argument-critical components. Do not reproduce familiar derivations unnecessarily. Explain key observations from tables and figures instead of repeating every number.

### Build understanding in reading order

Check what a target reader could know at this point from ordinary field knowledge and preceding manuscript content. Your access to later sections, code, and the full technical report does not give that information to the reader.

Before complex mechanisms, symbols, or detail, supply enough purpose, context, or overview to make their role understandable. Make relationships between paragraphs clear. Brief previews and cross-references are useful, but the central argument should not require repeated backward reading to discover why earlier material mattered.

Drafting may start with results; coherence checks use the final reading order. Track needed earlier explanations for their intended background/method location instead of stuffing all background into the results draft.

## Outline first, with an agent-written proposal

Use the technical reports to propose a complete argument outline without a long pre-outline interview. Present a short overall story: the research difficulty or gap, the approach, and the contribution and scope supported by available evidence.

For sections or key subsections, specify:

1. **Reader question:** what needs understanding or justification here?
2. **Intended answer:** what understanding or judgment should this section establish?
3. **Support:** which facts, findings, methods, or verified references support it?

This plans the argument; it does not prescribe three paragraphs per section. An explanatory method section can establish understanding rather than assert performance. Use claims commensurate with the evidence and record unresolved support where needed.

Put rough figure/table purpose, proposed content, and broad form alongside relevant evidence. The results narrative is established here, not repeated as a separate planning stage before prose. Point to run associations in the technical report rather than duplicating them in the outline.

Save the full proposal in `paper_outline.md`; show the overall story and a brief section overview in conversation, expanding areas the author wants to discuss. Apply their changes and check support if they introduce a new claim. Ask only consequential questions, not a confirmation survey for every outline cell.

## Drafting order follows the actual outline

Ask once whether the author prefers to start or end with particular material. Reuse an existing choice. Otherwise, prioritize:

- Research findings and evidence first.
- Methods and supporting material, including datasets, preprocessing, or experimental setup where needed.
- Interpretation, discussion, and conclusions.
- Background and positioning, including introduction and related work where present.
- Abstract and other whole-paper summaries last.

These are content functions, not mandatory sections. A dataset may be independent or part of another section; results and discussion may be merged. Draft within the agreed outline rather than creating headings to fit this sequence. Interleave and revise as useful; a part need not be fully final before another starts.

## Enter prose and work in parts

Briefly announce the current part, the agreed order, and the shared principles. Mention selected sample-paper practices when applicable and that unfinished images can use placeholders. Begin when the author instructs you to start, or continue under existing authorization. Do not repeat this permission request or the full principles before every paragraph.

Develop prose paragraph by paragraph within the current section. Adjust how many paragraphs are delivered together and how often the author reviews them to their preferences. Keep terminology, evidence, and links with neighboring paragraphs consistent. Before presenting a draft, revise it against the shared principles; make the revision, not merely a report of detected writing problems.

Apply actual selected style rules to prose and tables. Use real data and verified statistical conventions in tables. Keep figure placeholders compilable with stable labels and a short description of their purpose. Retain the relevant source and visual preferences for later drawing.

If the argument changes, update the outline; if facts or statistics change, update both technical reports; if the author establishes an ongoing preference, update the style document when present and workspace notes as needed. Avoid competing current instructions.

## Citations during drafting

Find, inspect, and insert references when a paragraph needs them. Maintain the manuscript bibliography as you go. Identify the actual paper and verify the metadata; inspect enough primary text to support the attached claim. A search result or a remembered citation is not automatically adequate support.

Record unresolved reference leads outside the finished prose and continue verification. Do not invent authors, titles, venues, DOIs, or results to fill gaps. Revise unsupported wording or identify the needed support. The final citation review is a second check, not a reason to postpone all citation work.
